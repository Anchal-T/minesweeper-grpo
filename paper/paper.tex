\begin{abstract}
We take GPT-2 (124M parameters) and specialize it in playing 6$\times$6 minesweeper
(6 mines) using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with verifiable,
programmatically-computed rewards. The full pipeline --- SFT behavior-cloning
warmup followed by on-policy GRPO --- runs on Tesla K80 GPUs (Kepler, sm\_37),
a hardware target that excludes modern frameworks: no torch~2.x, no vLLM, no
TRL. We therefore implement the GRPO training loop from scratch in
torch~1.13.1. Training a small policy with RL against a text-game environment
surfaced three failure modes that were silent or nearly silent at the metric
level but catastrophic for learning: an impure reward function that mutated
shared environment state across group rollouts, a train/inference position-id
mismatch for padded rows, and brittle answer parsing. Fixing these took mean
rollout reward from $-0.10$ (flat for hundreds of steps) to $+0.35$ and
climbing, with the bad-move rate falling from $55\%$ to $16\%$. We document
the training recipe, the failure modes, and a mine-count curriculum variant.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become the standard
recipe for specializing large language models on tasks with programmatic
ground truth: mathematics, code, and games. In this regime the reward is
computed by executing deterministic environment logic, so the learning signal
is exact; the difficulty lies entirely in the optimization. We study this
setting at the opposite end of the resource spectrum from typical RLVR work:
the policy is GPT-2~\cite{radford2019gpt2}, the accelerator is a Tesla K80
(2014, compute capability 3.7, 11\,GB GDDR5), and the software stack is frozen
at torch~1.13.1 + CUDA 11.7, the last build supporting the card. Everything
that modern RL stacks provide --- vLLM rollouts, TRL trainers, fused kernels,
bf16 --- is unavailable.

The task: given a text rendering of a mid-game minesweeper board, choose a
hidden cell that is safe. A move that reveals cells earns reward proportional
to the reveal; hitting a mine, clicking an open cell, or producing an
unparseable answer is penalized. The model must learn to read the numeric
constraints in the grid and act on them, entirely through scalar reward.

\section{Environment and Reward}
A board is a $6\times6$ grid with 6 mines (curriculum variant: 2--6). States
are rendered as whitespace-separated rows; hidden cells are `.' and revealed
cells show their adjacent-mine count. Each training prompt is a fresh random
mid-game state, produced by seeding one random safe cell and flood-filling, so
the state distribution matches genuine play.

The action is the model's completion of the prompt suffix
\texttt{Answer:}, e.g.\ \texttt{3,4}. The first \texttt{row,col} pair in the
completion (regex-parsed) is the move. The reward is verifiable and dense:
\begin{equation}
r(s,a) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
-1.0 & \text{mine, off-board, or unparseable}\\
-0.5 & \text{clicked an already-revealed cell}\\
0.5 + 0.5\cdot \dfrac{n_{\text{revealed}}(s,a)}{|H(s)|} & \text{safe click},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\text{revealed}}$ is the flood-fill gain and $H(s)$ the hidden set.
Full-game evaluation plays greedily (or at temperature $\tau$) until the board
is solved, a mine is hit, or a move limit is reached, and reports win rate and
cleared fraction.

\section{Training Pipeline}
\paragraph{SFT warmup.} We first behavior-clone a ground-truth expert (the
safe cell whose flood fill reveals the most, with deterministic row-major
tie-breaking) on random mid-game states: 600 steps, batch 32--64, lr
$10^{-5}$, fp16 autocast with gradient scaling. Random tie-breaking was
intentionally removed: with many equally-good cells, inconsistent targets
kept the SFT loss above $0.95$ and produced a degenerate greedy policy.
\paragraph{GRPO.} Each step samples $P{=}12$ fresh boards and $G{=}8$
completions per board from the current policy (temperature 1.0). Rewards are
group-normalized,
\begin{equation}
A_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r_{1..G})}{\mathrm{std}(r_{1..G}) + \epsilon},
\end{equation}
and the policy gradient $-\sum_i \sum_t A_i \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t})$ is
applied over answer tokens only, with AdamW (lr $10^{-5}$), gradient clipping
at 1.0, and an optional KL penalty to the frozen reference policy
($\beta{=}0.05$). Because rollouts and training share the same weights
in-process, no importance-ratio correction is needed for one inner epoch.
Updates are microbatched (24 rows) to fit K80 memory alongside co-tenant jobs.

\section{Three Nearly-Silent Failure Modes}
\subsection{Impure reward across group rollouts}
The reward function originally computed the flood-fill gain by revealing
cells \emph{on the shared board}. All $G$ rollouts of a group score the same
nominal state, so sample $k$'s reward was computed on the board mutated by
samples $1..k{-}1$: a cell safe for sample 1 scored as a wasted click for
sample 5. Identical actions received order-dependent rewards, advantages were
pure noise correlated with sample order, and mean reward sat flat at
$-0.10$ for hundreds of steps while the trainer appeared healthy. The fix ---
score every rollout against an untouched probe copy --- was a five-line
change and immediately moved mean reward from $-0.10$ to $+0.35$ (Figure
placeholder). \emph{Lesson: reward functions must be pure with respect to
state when multiple actions are scored against one state; mutation bugs in
the reward are invisible in the loss curve.}

\subsection{Position-id mismatch for padded rows}
HF \texttt{generate} computes position ids from the attention mask cumsum,
correctly handling left-padded batches. Our training-side log-prob path did
not pass position ids, so GPT-2 received \texttt{arange} positions: for every
row shorter than the batch max, answer tokens sat at shifted positions with a
gap after the context padding. The policy trained under a distribution shift
it never saw at inference. Passing
$\mathrm{pos} = \mathrm{cumsum}(\mathrm{mask}) - 1$ in training (matching
\texttt{generate}) lifted full-game clearing from $44\%$ to $64\%$ of safe
cells at equal training budget. \emph{Lesson: any hand-rolled scoring path
must replicate \texttt{generate}'s position handling exactly.}

\subsection{Brittle answer parsing}
The completion is sampled greedily in structure but the model repeats
patterns: \texttt{2,1,1,4,} with no whitespace. A parser demanding exactly
two comma-separated integers per whitespace token rejected every completion,
so all rewards were $-1$, all group variances zero, and all advantages zero:
GRPO silently became the identity update. Regex-parsing the first
\texttt{d+,d+} pair fixed it. \emph{Lesson: with verifiable rewards, the
parser is part of the reward; test it on real model output, not idealized
strings.}

We additionally observed fp16 gradient-scaler skips (normal, transient),
mode collapse without a KL anchor (irreversible within 100 steps at lr
$3{\times}10^{-5}$), and GPT-2's missing pad token.

\section{Hardware and Throughput}
All runs share the machine with unrelated co-tenant jobs. Measured on K80:
GPT-2 fp16 eager generation at batch 96$\times$90 tokens, SFT $\approx$5\,s/step
(batch 32), GRPO $\approx$12--17\,s/step (96 rollouts + update), limited by
co-tenant memory pressure and kernel-launch overhead on the 124M model.
Rolling out in fp16 autocast rather than fp32 roughly halved generation cost.

\section{Conclusion}
A 2014 GPU and a 2022 torch build are sufficient to run GRPO on a small LM
against a verifiable game environment, provided the trainer is written from
scratch and its assumptions are instrumented. The dominant risks are not
exotic: they are impure reward functions, position-id mismatches, and brittle
output parsing --- each invisible in the loss curve and each fixable in
minutes once observed in rollout diagnostics.
